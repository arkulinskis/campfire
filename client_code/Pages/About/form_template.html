How it works

A fire you can sit by.

Campfire isn't a video and it isn't a loop. Every flame you see is computed while you watch, from heat, fuel and air. Every crackle is made on the spot. Nothing about tonight's fire has happened before.

What you are looking at

Flames that flow

The flames are a fluid simulation running on your graphics card. Hot gas rises, swirls and pulls in cool air; wood gas burns where it meets oxygen; soot glows, then burns out at the tips. It runs in several thin layers that face you, a method from film effects work, so you see depth without the cost of a full 3D simulation.

The colours of real fire

Glowing soot takes its colour from its temperature: Planck's law for a black body, seen through the standard response of the human eye (CIE 1931). That is why the heart of the fire reads yellow-white and the tips deep orange. Close to the wood, a faint blue shows where fresh gas burns before soot forms.

Light from the flames themselves

The fire lights the camp. The simulation's brightness is measured every couple of frames, and that is what moves the light and the shadows on the stones, the ground and the trees. Your eyes adjust as well: when the fire flares, the night around it gets darker.

Wood that really burns

Every log has a kind of wood, some moisture and a weight. Heat from the flames and the coals dries it, lights it, chars it into glowing cracks and slowly eats it. A teepee falls in when its legs burn through. Logs collapse into the coal bed, and the coals cool over half an hour if nobody tends them.

Sound made live

There are no recordings. Each crackle is a tiny burst of shaped noise, timed by a random process that excites itself: every pop makes another a little more likely for a moment, which is how real fires bunch their crackles. The low roar follows the flames' own flicker. Resin whistles; damp wood hisses.

Chance, every time

The fire takes its randomness from your browser's cryptographic source. Where each flame starts on each log, when the wood pops, when the wind gusts: all of it is decided on the spot. You can't watch the same fire twice.

Three places

Things to try

  • Add a log and watch it smoke, then catch. Pine pops and throws sparks; birch flares; maple and mesquite burn slow and leave deep coals.
  • Put on a damp branch. It hisses and steams before it gives in.
  • Stir the coals (or tap the flames): sparks fly and the embers brighten.
  • Walk around the fire by dragging; scroll or pinch to lean in.
  • Fall asleep to it. The sleep timer lets the fire burn down and fades the sound away.
  • Leave it on. "Keep the fire going" adds a log whenever it burns low.
  • Keys: L adds a log, S stirs, M turns the sound off and on, F is full screen, P changes place.

Questions

Is this a video?

No. Nothing is recorded or looped. The flames, the light, the sparks and every sound are computed in your browser while you watch.

Why does my fire go out?

Because the wood burns. Each log lasts about ten to twenty minutes, depending on the wood. Add another when the fire burns low, or turn on "Keep the fire going". If it goes out completely, strike another match.

Can I fall asleep to it?

Yes. Open the settings and pick 20, 45 or 90 minutes: the fire is left to burn down and the sound fades away over the last five minutes. The fire keeps crackling in a background tab, too.

Will it drain my battery?

It uses the graphics chip, so it uses more power than a web page usually does. If a device struggles, the picture quality steps down by itself. "Light" in the settings is the gentlest.

Does it work on a phone?

Yes, on recent phones and tablets with WebGL 2 (current Safari, Chrome, Firefox and Edge). Headphones make the sound much better.

What does "others by a fire right now" mean?

While the fire burns, your browser tells the server once a minute that someone is sitting by a fire, using a random code made fresh for that visit. The server only counts those codes. Nothing about you is kept, and the codes are erased within the hour.

Is anything recorded about me?

No account, no microphone, no camera. Your place and settings are saved in your own browser. See Privacy & Terms at the bottom of the page.

The craft behind it

Campfire stands on decades of work by people who studied fire, fluids, light and sound:

  • Jos Stam, Stable Fluids, SIGGRAPH 1999: the fluid solver.
  • Ronald Fedkiw, Jos Stam and Henrik Wann Jensen, Visual Simulation of Smoke, SIGGRAPH 2001: vorticity confinement, the small swirls.
  • Andrew Selle and others, An Unconditionally Stable MacCormack Method, 2008: sharp flame edges.
  • Christopher Horvath and Willi Geiger, Directable, High-Resolution Simulation of Fire on the GPU, SIGGRAPH 2009: fire in camera-facing slices.
  • Andy Farnell, Designing Sound, MIT Press 2010: crackling, hissing and lapping, built from scratch.
  • Chris Wyman, Peter-Pike Sloan and Peter Shirley, Simple Analytic Approximations to the CIE XYZ Color Matching Functions, 2013: the colours of glowing soot.
  • Jorge Jimenez, Next Generation Post Processing in Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare, SIGGRAPH 2014: the glow around bright flames.
  • Troy Sobotka's AgX view transform: how bright light turns to film.
  • Amos Dolbear, The Cricket as a Thermometer, 1897.
  • three.js, the open-source 3D library the scene is drawn with (MIT licence).
